## About This Project

**GWAS Protocol** is an evolving, open educational resource designed to provide a systematic understanding of genome-wide association studies (GWAS), from fundamental concepts in human genetics and genetic epidemiology to practical statistical analysis and interpretation of genomic findings.

The project emphasizes both **theoretical understanding and hands-on implementation**, with explanations of why each analytical step is necessary, how it is performed, and how its results should be interpreted.

Our objective is to bridge the gap between conceptual knowledge and practical GWAS analysis, particularly for researchers and students beginning their work in statistical genetics and computational genomics.

## Key Features

- **Step-by-step explanations:** Concepts are introduced progressively, from introductory genetics to advanced GWAS methodology.
- **Statistical foundations:** Genetic association models, hypothesis testing, effect estimates, statistical significance, and interpretation.
- **Practical workflows:** Command-line and R-based examples illustrating GWAS analytical procedures.
- **Genotype quality control:** Principles and methods for identifying problematic samples and genetic variants.
- **Reproducibility:** Emphasis on documenting commands, software versions, quality-control decisions, and analytical outputs.
- **Biological interpretation:** Connecting statistical associations to genetic and biological questions.
- **Progressive learning:** Material structured to support both independent study and classroom instruction.

## Who Is This Resource For?

This resource is intended for:

- Graduate students and early-career researchers in genetics, genomics, and bioinformatics.
- Researchers in genetic epidemiology, public health, and biomedical sciences.
- Computational biologists and data scientists interested in genetic association studies.
- Professionals seeking a practical introduction to GWAS analytical methods.

No advanced GWAS experience is assumed for the introductory chapters. Familiarity with basic statistics, genetics, and R is helpful.

## Topics Covered and Planned

| Learning area | Major topics |
|---|---|
| GWAS fundamentals | Genetic variation, SNPs, alleles, genotype data, and GWAS principles |
| Study design | Case-control studies, quantitative traits, phenotypes, and covariates |
| Statistical methods | Linear regression, logistic regression, and genetic association models |
| Genotype quality control | Sample-level and variant-level quality-control procedures |
| Population structure | Ancestry, population stratification, relatedness, and principal components |
| Association analysis | GWAS testing, effect estimates, and statistical interpretation |
| Results and visualization | Manhattan plots, Q–Q plots, and interpretation of association signals |
| Advanced methods | Imputation, downstream analysis, and other methodological extensions |

**Note:** This is a developing educational project. Some topics are available in published chapters, while others are planned for future additions.

## Software and Analytical Tools

The tutorials introduce tools commonly used in GWAS research, including:

- **R:** Statistical analysis, data processing, and visualization.
- **PLINK:** Genotype data management, quality control, and association testing.
- **Command-line environments:** File inspection, data manipulation, and reproducible workflows.

Additional tools may be introduced as the project progresses.

## Getting Started

The easiest way to use this resource is through the published online book:

**[Open the GWAS Protocol Book](https://sansin5982.github.io/GWAS-Protocol/)**

We recommend beginning with the introductory chapters and progressing sequentially through study design, statistical methods, genotype quality control, and association analysis.

Where practical examples are provided, we recommend recording the input data, software versions, commands, parameters, outputs, and analytical decisions to support reproducibility.

## Reproducibility and Data Use

Examples may use simulated teaching datasets, publicly accessible research datasets, or illustrative calculations, depending on the chapter.

Simulated examples should not be interpreted as empirical research findings. Real-data analyses should document dataset provenance, access conditions, reference genome builds, sample and variant counts, and quality-control decisions.
